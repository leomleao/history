# Reference: Voyage Route, Ports of Call & Steerage Hardships

**Vessel**: Steamship *Colombo* (former *Brazil*, 2,408 GRT, 104 m length, 10 m beam, 9 knots)  
**Route Pattern**: Genoa → Lisbon → Mindelo (Cape Verde) → Rio de Janeiro → Santos  
**Date Range**: October / November 1891 (arriving at Hospedaria do Brás, São Paulo, on 15 December 1891)

---

## 1. Port of Embarkation: Genoa (Gênova)

- **Associated Photographic Asset**: [`../images/genoa-port-1892-noack.webp`](file:///Users/leo/dev/history/agy-videos/01-fosca-at-sea/images/genoa-port-1892-noack.webp)  
  *Alfred Noack’s 1892 panoramic photograph of the crowded port of Genoa.*
- **Historical Atmosphere**:
  - The bustling quays of Genoa were a sensory shock for rural peasant families from Veneto. Thousands of emigrants from Northern and Southern Italy converged with woven baskets, wooden trunks, and crying children.
  - Middlemen, steamship ticket agents (*sensali*), health inspectors, and police crowded the stone piers.
  - The towering black hull of the *Colombo*, with its single tall iron funnel belching coal smoke, represented both an escape from flood-ruined Veneto and an irreversible leap into the abyss.

## 2. Ports of Call Across the Atlantic

### Lisbon (Lisboa) — Cais do Sodré
- **Associated Photographic Asset**: [`../images/lisbon-cais-do-sodre-1890.webp`](file:///Users/leo/dev/history/agy-videos/01-fosca-at-sea/images/lisbon-cais-do-sodre-1890.webp)  
  *Period view of the Tagus waterfront and Cais do Sodré anchorage, c. 1890–1895.*
- **Significance**:
  - The regular service of Giacomo Cresta's line called at Lisbon to take on mail, provisions, and Portuguese emigrants.
  - For the Italian families on board, watching the hills of Lisbon fade into the distance marked the final sighting of continental Europe. Beyond lay thousands of miles of unbroken ocean.

### Cape Verde — Porto Grande at Mindelo (São Vicente)
- **Associated Photographic Asset**: [`../images/cape-verde-porto-grande-1890.webp`](file:///Users/leo/dev/history/agy-videos/01-fosca-at-sea/images/cape-verde-porto-grande-1890.webp)  
  *Historic view of the arid, volcanic Porto Grande bay at Mindelo, c. 1890.*
- **Significance**:
  - A crucial Atlantic coaling stop. Steamships like the *Colombo* required coal bunkers topped up before the long southern run to the Equator and South America.
  - The stark, barren volcanic peaks of São Vicente, the suffocating black coal dust from lighter barges, and the equatorial heat gave emigrants an eerie, foreign foretaste of an alien world. Passengers were confined to the ship or exposed to harsh tropical sun.

## 3. The Physical Hardships Aboard the Colombo

- **The Narrow Hull's Brutal Motion**:
  - With a length of 104 meters but a beam (width) of only 10 meters, the *Colombo* was narrow and rolled severely in heavy Atlantic swells. In a 20-degree roll, standing upright without bracing was impossible.
- **The Steerage Hold**:
  - 700 third-class passengers were packed into converted cargo holds with double- or triple-tiered wooden bunks and straw mattresses.
  - Air was thick with fumes from coal engines, bilge water, stale food, vomit, and vinegar disinfectants. Companionway hatches were battened down during squalls, trapping families in total darkness illuminated only by swaying oil lanterns.
- **The Threat of Contagion**:
  - Only ten months earlier, in February 1891, an outbreak of yellow fever aboard this exact ship had killed 15 passengers on a northbound run. Disease, dehydration, and infant fevers were constant terrors for a mother carrying a 1-year-old baby and five young children.
- **The Emotional Toll**:
  - The agonizing uncertainty: *Did we make the right choice? Will the children survive the crossing? What awaits us on the other side?*
