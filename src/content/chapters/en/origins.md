## A name fixed to a place

The family story begins with a line in an Italian certificate. Giuseppe Giacomo Panzonato was born on 22 July 1856 in Gambarare, now part of Mira, west of Venice. The record names his parents as Domenico Panzonato and Angela Salviato, *detta Campanella*. In a few words, the [birth record](/en/archive/giuseppe-birth-1856/) joins a child, two parents, and the village that would remain central to the family story.

Later Brazilian documents reshape these names. Giuseppe becomes José; Panzonato appears as Pansonatto or Pansonato; Domenico and Angela acquire new spellings. The 1856 certificate gives the story its earliest clear point of reference.

## Domenico, Angela, and “Campanella”

The phrase *detta Campanella* follows Angela Salviato’s name. In records of the region, *detta* marked another name by which someone or a family branch was known. It preserves a trace of how Angela was identified within her community.

Domenico and Angela enter the exhibition through their son’s certificate. Their names connect Giuseppe to an earlier generation in Gambarare and will echo, sometimes altered, in the Brazilian records of their grandchildren.

## Two families meet in Mira

On 20 November 1881, Giuseppe Panzonato married Fosca Moro in Mira. The civil extract identifies act 60, part I, for that year. The [marriage record](/en/archive/giuseppe-fosca-marriage-1881/) is the moment when the Panzonato and Moro lines first appear together on a dated page.

Other family records identify Fosca’s parents as Andrea Moro and Pasqua, or Paschoa, Toffano. Fosca and Giuseppe would remain together through the move to Brazil, where their names appear side by side again a decade later.

## Six children before the crossing

By 1891, the couple had a household of eight. Giuseppe and Fosca travelled with Luigia, ten; Catarina, nine; Candida, seven; Luigi, five; Domenico, three; and Eugenio, one. The ages in the immigration register turn a family group into a sequence of lives, from the eldest child approaching adolescence to the youngest still an infant.

Their [São Paulo entry](/en/archive/panzonato-hospedaria-1891/) shows that the ocean journey belonged to parents and children together. These eight names will form the centre of the next chapter.

## The Veneto around them

The opening photograph shows a working street in Venice in 1891. People gather between worn walls while baskets, clothing, and everyday traffic fill the narrow *calle*. It offers [a view of the wider region](/en/archive/venice-1891-working-calle/) in the year the family emigrated; the scene is Venetian context rather than a portrait of Gambarare or its residents.

Agrarian inquiries from the period describe rural communities facing cheap imported grain, taxation, and weak public support. This [regional setting](/en/archive/context-italian-agrarian-inquiry/) belonged to the world around many Veneto families. Beside the urban photograph, the contemporary Dolo view in the gallery brings the canals, towers, and low horizon of the Brenta landscape into the story.

## Toward 1891

The surviving Italian sequence is concise: Giuseppe’s birth in Gambarare, his parents’ names, the marriage to Fosca in Mira, and a household that grew to include six children. On 15 December 1891, that family appears together in a São Paulo immigration register associated with the steamship *Colombo*. Gambarare would reappear years later in a Brazilian marriage record, carried across the Atlantic as a daughter’s place of birth.
