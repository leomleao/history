## Eight people in one row

On 15 December 1891, a clerk entered an entire household into a ruled table: Giuseppe, 35; Fosca, 30; Luigia, 10; Catarina, 9; Candida, 7; Luigi, 5; Domenico, 3; and Eugenio, 1. The surname is Panzonato and the vessel is the *Colombo*.

The [Hospedaria entry](/en/archive/panzonato-hospedaria-1891/) gives the crossing a human scale. Six children are arranged by age beneath their parents; the youngest is one year old. This single family row is the clearest surviving view of who travelled together.

## Rio behind them, Tietê ahead

Two fields turn the entry into a map. The family is recorded as *procedente de Rio de Janeiro*—proceeding from Rio de Janeiro—and its destination is *Tietê*. A later [certified copy](/en/archive/panzonato-desembarque-certificate-2002/) repeats those details and identifies the archival location as book 031, page 283, order 07264.

Rio de Janeiro is therefore the last recorded provenance, São Paulo the place of registration, and Tietê the destination written by the clerk. The record begins the family’s Brazilian geography without supplying a European embarkation port or a day-by-day itinerary.

## The ship before it was the Colombo

The vessel had already crossed one historical threshold. Built in 1873 as the *Brazil*, it was acquired, converted, and renamed *Colombo* in 1888. A [history of the ship](/en/archive/context-colombo-history/) describes regular service linking Genoa, Lisbon, Rio de Janeiro, and Santos.

Its scale was considerable: about 700 passengers in common accommodation, with another 80 to 100 in cabins. Refrigerated storage allowed fresh provisions to be carried, while the same account describes immigrant transport conditions as extremely precarious. Those details give the *Colombo* its own biography alongside the family names in the register.

## A period image of steerage

Arnaldo Ferraguti’s 1889 illustration shows an emigrant family resting among bundles on an open deck. Created for Edmondo De Amicis’s *Sull’Oceano*, it evokes the world of mass departure close to the date of the Panzonato journey. The gallery’s [steerage illustration from 1891](/en/archive/steerage-interior-1891/) opens another contemporary view: passengers and luggage gathered in a crowded compartment.

Both images depict other travellers and routes, showing the spaces, crowds, and luggage of ocean migration in the same period.

## A ticket as an administrative object

The [third-class ticket from 1910](/en/archive/sacop-third-class-ticket-1910/) belongs to a later traveller. Its printed rules, stamps, narrow format, and handwritten annotations reveal how a journey could be translated into an official object. It also points to the expanding systems of subsidized passage that connected shipping companies, migrants, and the São Paulo government.

Placed beside the Panzonato register, the ticket shows two sides of migration paperwork: the document carried through a journey and the ledger created on arrival.

## A new documentary life

The register captures the family after the ocean crossing and at the start of a new sequence of records. Catarina will later be identified in Capivari as a native of Gambarare. Luigi becomes Luiz in Brazilian documents. Giuseppe becomes José. The crossing is visible not only as movement between continents, but as the moment when eight Italian names begin to change on Brazilian pages.
