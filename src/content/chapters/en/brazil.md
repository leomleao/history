## The destination in the ledger

At the right edge of the family’s 1891 entry is one decisive word: *Tietê*. It is the immediate destination recorded for Giuseppe, Fosca, and their six children. The [family entry](/en/archive/panzonato-hospedaria-1891/) begins their Brazilian story with a direction.

The next firm family event is eight years later in Capivari. The route and years between the two places remain a short documentary gap; from 1899 onward, births, marriages, occupations, and deaths begin to give the family a sustained inland address.

## A hostel built for mass arrival

The Hospedaria de Imigrantes in São Paulo [began receiving immigrants in 1887](https://museudaimigracao.org.br/exposicoes/temporarias/hospedaria-130) beside the railway. Designed for about 4,000 people, it combined registration offices, kitchens, refectories, dormitories, medical facilities, and an area where employers contracted workers. Courtyard and railway photographs show an institution built to receive people in large numbers.

The wider [São Paulo immigration system](/en/archive/context-apesp-immigration/) linked subsidized passages to the province’s demand for labour. It also produced contracts, debts, penalties, disputes, and movement between farms. The Panzonato entry places one named household inside that vast operation.

## Rails into the province

Railways connected the capital’s hostel with towns and agricultural districts inland. Capivari had been served since 1875 by the Ituana network, which carried passengers and produce across the province. The [station history](/en/archive/context-capivari-station/) follows that connection through later decades.

The station in the gallery was photographed in 1981, and its building dates from 1918. It shows the enduring rail site in the town where the family’s Brazilian records accumulated.

## Capivari: sugar, coffee, and work

Capivari’s economy rested on more than one crop. Sugar had long shaped the region, while coffee was present by the nineteenth century and increasingly tied farms to rail and immigrant labour. Period photographs from the São Paulo state archive show the wider agricultural landscape: harvest work, drying terraces, and workers’ houses.

Giuseppe’s death record later calls him a *lavrador*. His son José is given the same occupation in 1921. Their records place two generations in agricultural work within a town shaped by both sugar and coffee.

## A Brazilian-born son

On 18 November 1899, José Pansonatto Filho was born in Capivari. His [birth certificate](/en/archive/jose-filho-birth-1899/) names José Pansonato and Fosca Moro as his parents and records all four grandparents. The Italian-born couple now appears on the page as the parents of a Brazilian-born child.

The town returns in other milestones. Catarina married Luigi Sari there on 8 February 1902. José Filho married Assumpta Elvira Pozzatto there on 4 June 1921. Fosca and Giuseppe would both die in Capivari. Each event adds another dated point to the family’s relationship with the place.

## The world of the colono

A later [oral-history interview](/en/archive/context-colonato-oral-history/) with another São Paulo *colono* recalls responsibility for 2,000 coffee trees per family, payments during the year and a final harvest reckoning. The worker named the tools families bought—hoes, rakes, sieves—and remembered reused sacks and houses with beaten-earth floors.

It is an attributed voice from the wider labour system, paired here with agricultural images from the state archive. Around the Panzonatos’ own dates and occupations, it restores the material world of work: soil, tools, housing, payment, and the rhythm of harvest.
